Section 2.2 · Formatting Elements

<strong> · <b> · <em> · <i>

Four elements, two visual effects (bold and italic), four very different meanings. Pick the right one and your page becomes accessible, SEO-friendly, and machine-readable. Pick wrong and you leave information on the table.

1 The Four Elements

HTML provides four elements that map onto two visual effects. Two of them carry semantic meaning; the other two are purely stylistic. Choosing the right one matters because screen readers, search engines, and other tools rely on the meaning.

<strong>

Importance. Bold by default. Screen reader may emphasize.

<b>

Stylistic bold without importance. Used for keywords, product names.

<em>

Stress emphasis. Italic by default. Changes sentence meaning.

<i>

Alternate voice / idiomatic text. Italic by default. Taxonomy, foreign terms.

ElementDefault lookSemantic meaningWhen NOT to use
<strong>BoldImportant, urgent, seriousJust for visual boldness
<b>BoldNone (stylistic only)If the text is truly important — use <strong>
<em>ItalicStress emphasisFor book titles — use <cite>
<i>ItalicAlternate voice, technical term, foreign phraseFor emphasis — use <em>

2 <strong> — Importance

The <strong> element indicates that its contents are of strong importance, seriousness, or urgency. Browsers render it as bold by default, but the meaning goes beyond visual weight.

<p>
  <strong>Warning:</strong> Deleting your account cannot be undone.
</p>

<p>
  You must <strong>verify your email</strong> before signing in.
</p>

Warning: Deleting your account cannot be undone.

You must verify your email before signing in.

Nesting <strong> inside <strong>

Nesting increases the level of importance. The outer marks something as important; the inner marks it as even more important. Some browsers and screen readers increase the visual emphasis accordingly.

<p>
  <strong>Do not<strong> ever</strong> share your password.</strong>
</p>
Pro Tip Use <strong> for content the reader must not miss: warnings, deadlines, must-do requirements. For "highlighted keyword" use cases (like product names in marketing copy), prefer <b> or <mark>.

3 <b> — Stylistic Offset

The <b> element draws attention to text for utilitarian purposes without conveying extra importance. It is the "stylistic bold" — useful when you want bold text but the content is not more important than its surroundings.

Common use cases

  • Article lead-in keywords ("In summary…")
  • Product names in marketing copy
  • The first word in a list item that acts as a label
  • Document abstract keywords
<p>
  <b>Ingredients:</b> flour, sugar, eggs, butter.
</p>

<p>
  <b>Acme X100</b> headphones deliver 40-hour battery life.
</p>

Ingredients: flour, sugar, eggs, butter.

Acme X100 headphones deliver 40-hour battery life.

Common Mistake In HTML 4, <b> meant simply "bold". In HTML5 it was redefined as "stylistic offset without importance". If the text genuinely matters, use <strong>. Reserve <b> for keywords, product names, and lead-in labels.

4 <em> — Stress Emphasis

The <em> element represents stress emphasis — the kind of emphasis that would change the meaning of a spoken sentence. Saying the emphasized word louder or with a different pitch.

<p>Call me <em>later</em>.</p>      <!-- not now -->
<p><em>Call</em> me later.</p>      <!-- don't text me -->
<p>Call <em>me</em> later.</p>      <!-- not her -->

Call me later.

Call me later.

Call me later.

Notice how each version implies a different spoken emphasis, and therefore a slightly different meaning. That is the power of <em>.

Nesting <em>

Nested <em> elements indicate greater levels of stress. Browsers typically render the inner element with normal weight (counteracting the italic) to maintain readability while still conveying stress.

<p>I <em>really<em> really</em> need</em> this done.</p>

5 <i> — Idiomatic / Alternate Voice

The <i> element represents text in an alternate voice or mood, or otherwise offset from normal prose. Common cases:

  • Taxonomic designations (Escherichia coli)
  • Foreign phrases (je ne sais quoi)
  • Internal monologue or thought
  • Ship names (HMS Beagle)
  • Technical terms being introduced
<p>
  The bacterium <i>Escherichia coli</i> is commonly
  found in the lower intestine.
</p>

<p>
  She had a certain <i lang="fr">je ne sais quoi</i>.
</p>

<p>
  Darwin sailed on the <i>HMS Beagle</i> in 1831.
</p>

The bacterium Escherichia coli is commonly found in the lower intestine.

She had a certain je ne sais quoi.

Darwin sailed on the HMS Beagle in 1831.

Info When using <i> for a foreign phrase, always add the lang attribute so screen readers and translation tools handle it correctly.

7 Nesting & Combined Use

These elements can be nested to combine meanings. The most common combinations:

<!-- Important AND emphasized -->
<p>You <strong><em>must</em></strong> read the contract.</p>

<!-- A foreign phrase that is also important -->
<p>Remember the phrase <strong><i lang="la">carpe diem</i></strong>.</p>

<!-- A keyword label with an emphasized sub-part -->
<p><b>Price:</b> the <em>final</em> cost is $99.</p>
Pro Tip When nesting <strong> inside <em> or vice versa, order them so the outermost matches the broader meaning. Usually <strong> wraps <em>, since importance is the larger category.

8 Examples Across Languages

English

<p>The <strong>deadline</strong> is <em>tomorrow</em>.</p>

French

<p lang="fr">La <strong>date limite</strong> est <em>demain</em>.</p>

Spanish

<p lang="es">La <strong>fecha límite</strong> es <em>mañana</em>.</p>

Arabic (RTL)

<p dir="rtl" lang="ar">الموعد النهائي <strong>غدا</strong>.</p>

Chinese (no plural/spaces)

<p lang="zh">截止日期是<strong>明天</strong>。</p>

Japanese

<p lang="ja">締め切りは<strong>明日</strong>です。</p>
Info Bold and italic styling works across languages, but be aware: in CJK scripts, "italic" often doesn't have a natural equivalent and may fall back to a different font. Test your design across languages.

9 Accessibility

Screen readers handle these elements differently:

  • <strong> — Some screen readers (NVDA, JAWS) increase voice pitch or volume slightly to convey importance. This behavior is configurable by the user.
  • <em> — Most screen readers apply stress to emphasized words, similar to spoken emphasis.
  • <b> and <i> — No semantic meaning is announced. They are purely visual.
Common Mistake Don't rely on bold/italic alone to convey meaning. If a warning is critical, mark it with <strong>, not just <b>. And never use color changes alone as the only signal — always pair with semantic markup.

10 Styling Tips

Most projects leave the defaults alone — bold and italic are universally understood. But you can refine them:

strong, b {
  font-weight: 700;   /* slightly bolder than default 600 */
}

em, i {
  font-style: italic;
}

/* Use a different weight for emphasized text inside strong */
strong em, strong i { font-weight: 600; }

/* Reset italic for CJK where italic doesn't render well */
:lang(zh) i, :lang(ja) i, :lang(ko) i {
  font-style: normal;
  font-weight: 600;     /* use weight instead */
  color: #7c3aed;
}
Pro Tip For uppercase headings like SECTION 1, you may want to disable the default bold on <b> so it inherits normal weight: h2 b { font-weight: inherit; }.

11 Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy It HurtsFix
Using <b> for warnings No semantic importance; screen readers skip Use <strong>
Using <i> for emphasis No stress meaning announced Use <em>
Using <em> for book titles Book titles aren't stress emphasis Use <cite>
Using <span style="font-weight:bold"> Loses semantic info; harder to style consistently Use <strong> or <b>
Nesting <b> inside <strong> redundantly Double-bold adds nothing Pick one
Forgetting lang on foreign <i> Wrong pronunciation; translation tools confused Add lang="xx"
Using bold/italic alone for meaning Color-blind & low-vision users miss it Pair with <strong>/<em> or icons

12 Try It Yourself

Exercise 1 — Pick the right element For each sentence below, choose <strong>, <b>, <em>, or <i>:
  • "The bacterium Staphylococcus aureus…" — italicize the Latin name
  • "You must submit by Friday" — emphasize "must"
  • "Price: $99" — bold label, no extra importance
  • "The ship Endeavour set sail in 1768" — italic ship name
  • "Warning: high voltage" — important warning

<p>
  <span style="font-weight:bold">Note:</span>
  The book <span style="font-style:italic">Dune</span>
  was written by Frank Herbert. You <span style="font-weight:bold">must</span>
  read it before next week.
</p>

13 Summary

  • <strong> = importance, urgency, seriousness. Bold by default.
  • <b> = stylistic bold for keywords/labels, no importance.
  • <em> = stress emphasis that changes spoken meaning. Italic by default.
  • <i> = alternate voice: foreign terms, taxonomic names, ship names, thoughts.
  • Screen readers may add pitch/volume changes for <strong> and <em>, but not for <b>/<i>.
  • For book and film titles, use <cite> instead of <i>.
  • Always add lang when using <i> for foreign phrases.
