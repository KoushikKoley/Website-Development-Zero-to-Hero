<main>, <article> & <aside>

Three elements that define the content regions of a page. <main> holds the page's unique primary content (exactly one per page). <article> holds self-contained, syndicatable content. <aside> holds tangentially related content. Get these right and your page communicates its structure to browsers, screen readers, and search engines alike.

1 The <main> element

<main> represents the dominant content of the page — the stuff that is unique to this URL and wouldn't be repeated on another page. Screen readers expose it as a "main" landmark; users press one key to jump straight to it, bypassing header, nav, and footer.

<body>
  <header>…site chrome…</header>
  <nav>…primary nav…</nav>

  <main>
    <h1>About Acme</h1>
    <p>We make widgets…</p>
    <!-- the actual content of the /about page -->
  </main>

  <footer>…copyright…</footer>
</body>

Before <main> was added to HTML in 2013, developers bolted a "skip to main content" link at the top of every page. The <main> element makes that link redundant — the landmark itself is the skip target.

Pro Tip The implicit ARIA role of <main> is main. Don't add role="main" to it — it's already there, and adding it explicitly is an ARIA anti-pattern (the First Rule of ARIA: don't use ARIA to do what HTML already does).

2 Rules for <main>

RuleWhy
Exactly one <main> per page The spec says so; multiple <main>s confuse screen readers about which is the dominant content
Must not be a descendant of <header>, <footer>, <nav>, <aside>, or <article> Those are all "chrome" or sub-content; <main> must be top-level content
Excludes site-wide navigation, copyright info, sidebars, and logos Those belong in <header>/<nav>/<footer>/<aside>, not <main>
Can contain <article>s (e.g. blog index) The articles themselves are the main content
In SPAs (React, Vue), keep <main> mounted across route changes So SR users keep their place; only swap content inside <main>
Common Mistake Wrapping the entire page (including header and footer) in <main>. The whole point of <main> is to mark what's unique to this URL. Header and footer repeat across every page; they don't belong inside <main>.

3 The <article> element

<article> represents a self-contained composition — content that could be distributed or syndicated independently of the rest of the page. The classic test: if you extracted this content and put it in an RSS feed, would it make sense on its own?

Appropriate uses

  • Blog posts, news articles, magazine pieces
  • Product detail cards (in a listing context)
  • Forum posts
  • User comments (each comment is its own article, often nested)
  • Interactive widgets that are self-contained (a stock ticker, a weather card)
  • Recipe pages, tutorial steps, documentation pages
<article>
  <header>
    <h1>How to Make Cold Brew Coffee</h1>
    <p>By <a rel="author" href="/authors/ada">Ada</a> ·
       <time datetime="2024-03-12">March 12, 2024</time></p>
  </header>

  <p>Cold brew is coffee steeped in cold water for 12-24 hours…</p>

  <h2>Ingredients</h2>
  <ul>
    <li>1 cup coarse-ground coffee</li>
    <li>4 cups cold water</li>
  </ul>

  <h2>Steps</h2>
  <ol>
    <li>Combine coffee and water in a jar.</li>
    <li>Refrigerate for 18 hours.</li>
    <li>Strain through a fine mesh.</li>
  </ol>

  <footer>
    <p>Tags: <a href="/tag/coffee">coffee</a>,
              <a href="/tag/recipe">recipe</a></p>
  </footer>
</article>

4 The syndication test

Ask yourself: "If I put this content in an RSS feed, or republished it on another site, would it make complete sense on its own?"

ContentSelf-contained?Use <article>?
A blog postYesYes
A news storyYesYes
A product card in a gridYes (it has its own title, image, price, link)Yes
A user commentYes (it's a self-contained post by another user)Yes (often nested inside the parent article)
The "About" section on a homepageNo (depends on surrounding context)Use <section> instead
A homepage hero with tagline + CTANo (it's an intro to the rest of the page)Use <section> or just a header
A sidebar widget showing recent postsNo (it's a navigation aid, not standalone content)Use <aside>
A recipeYesYes
An entire blog index pageThe page itself is not an article; each post preview isWrap each preview in <article>
Pro Tip An <article> can contain other <article>s — for example, a blog post article containing comment articles. The spec explicitly allows this nesting. It signals "these comments are themselves self-contained pieces, but they belong to this parent article".

5 The <aside> element

<aside> represents content that is tangentially related to the content around it. It's the "by the way" element — content that could be removed without breaking the main flow, but adds context, navigation, or related material.

Two main flavors

PositionMeaningExample
Outside <main> or <article> (sibling) Site-wide sidebar — related to the entire page Right rail on a news site with "Most Read" + ads
Inside an <article> Tangential to that article's specific content A pull-quote, a "did you know?" callout, a sidebar tip 
<!-- Site-wide aside -->
<main>
  <article>…main blog post…</article>
</main>
<aside aria-label="Sidebar">
  <section>
    <h2>Most Read</h2>
    <ul>…</ul>
  </section>
  <section>
    <h2>Newsletter Signup</h2>
    <form>…</form>
  </section>
</aside>

<!-- Article-internal aside (pull quote) -->
<article>
  <p>Cold brew coffee is made by steeping coarse-ground
     coffee in cold water for 12-24 hours. The result is
     a smoother, less acidic cup than hot-brewed coffee.</p>

  <aside>
    <p>"Cold brew has about 65% less acidity than hot brew."
       — <cite>Harvard Coffee Study, 2023</cite></p>
  </aside>

  <p>The long steeping time extracts different compounds…</p>
</article>

6 When to use <aside>

Use <aside> for…Don't use <aside> for…
Sidebars (related posts, tag clouds, recent comments) Footer link lists (use <nav> + <footer>)
Pull quotes inside an article The main body of an article (use <article>)
"Did you know?" callout boxes Section headings (use <h2>/<h3>)
Advertisements (when contextually related) Modal dialogs (use <dialog>)
Glossary or definition box next to a tutorial The tutorial itself
Author bio next to an article Article body
Table of contents for a long article The article's main content
Pro Tip Add an aria-label to a site-wide <aside> when there's more than one — e.g. aria-label="Related posts", aria-label="Newsletter signup". Without it, screen readers announce them all as "complementary landmark" and users can't tell them apart.

7 Composition patterns

Pattern A: Blog post with sidebar

<main>
  <article>
    <header>
      <h1>Post Title</h1>
      <p>By <a rel="author">Ada</a> on
         <time datetime="2024-03-12">March 12</time></p>
    </header>
    <p>Body…</p>
    <footer>Tags: …</footer>
  </article>
</main>

<aside aria-label="Sidebar">
  <section>
    <h2>Recent Posts</h2>
    <ul>…</ul>
  </section>
  <section>
    <h2>Categories</h2>
    <ul>…</ul>
  </section>
</aside>

Pattern B: Product listing page

<main>
  <h1>Coffee Makers</h1>

  <section aria-label="Product grid">
    <article>
      <h2><a href="/p/french-press">French Press</a></h2>
      <p>$24.99</p>
      <p>Classic 34oz glass press.</p>
    </article>

    <article>
      <h2><a href="/p/pour-over">Pour Over Kit</a></h2>
      <p>$39.99</p>
      <p>Glass carafe with stainless filter.</p>
    </article>

    <article>
      <h2><a href="/p/cold-brew">Cold Brew Maker</a></h2>
      <p>$32.00</p>
      <p>1.5L cold brew carafe.</p>
    </article>
  </section>
</main>

Pattern C: Article with nested comment articles

<article>
  <h1>Cold Brew Recipe</h1>
  <p>Body…</p>

  <section>
    <h2>Comments</h2>

    <article>
      <h3>Tried this — works great!</h3>
      <p>By Bob on March 13</p>
      <p>I added a splash of milk…</p>
    </article>

    <article>
      <h3>Question about ratios</h3>
      <p>By Carol on March 14</p>
      <p>Can I double the recipe?</p>
    </article>
  </section>
</article>

8 Common mistakes

MistakeWhy it's wrongFix
Two <main> elements on one page Spec violation; SR users can't tell which is dominant One <main> per page; merge the second into the first
Putting site nav inside <main> Nav is chrome, not unique content Move <nav> outside <main>, usually into <header>
Using <article> for every section just because it has a heading <article> is for self-contained, syndicatable content Use <section> for thematic groupings without syndication value
Forgetting aria-label on multiple <aside>s SR announces "complementary" with no distinguishing name Add aria-label="…" to each
Wrapping a single ad in <aside> deep inside an article If it's purely promotional and unrelated, it's not "tangential" Use a plain <div> with appropriate labeling
Using <aside> as a generic styling wrapper It carries the "complementary landmark" role — that may not be what you want Use <div> for purely visual wrappers
Nesting <main> inside <article> Spec violation: <main> must not be a descendant of <article> Swap them — <article> goes inside <main>

9 Try it yourself

Exercise 1 — Landmark audit Open a news article on a major site (e.g. BBC, NYT). Open DevTools' Accessibility tab. Identify the main, article, and aside landmarks. If any are missing or misused, write down what should change.
Exercise 2 — Syndication test Take a page you've built. For each major block of content, ask: "If I put this in an RSS feed alone, would it make sense?" Mark each block as Article, Section, Aside, or "remove". Refactor accordingly.
Exercise 3 — Build a product card Build a product card with image, name, price, "Add to cart" button. Wrap it in <article>. Why? Because a product card is self-contained and syndicatable — you could embed the same card on a partner site.
Exercise 4 — Pull quote aside Take a long article. Find a striking sentence. Wrap it in an <aside> with aria-label="Pull quote". Place it inline in the article body. Style it with CSS to look like a magazine pull quote. Verify screen readers announce it as a complementary region.

10 Browser support

Chrome 26+ Firefox 21+ Safari 7+ Edge 12+ All modern mobile browsers

All three elements have universal support in modern browsers. <main> was the last to be added (2013), but it's now supported everywhere — including the implicit main ARIA role exposed to screen readers.

Reader Mode, Pocket, and similar tools all key off <article> and <main> to extract content. A page built without them often can't be saved or read in reader mode at all.

11 Real-world use cases

News sites

Article in <article>, related stories in <aside>, ad blocks in <aside> with aria-label="Advertisement".

Blog themes

<main> wraps post + comments; sidebar is <aside>.

E-commerce product pages

Product details as <article>, "related products" as <aside>.

E-commerce listings

Each product card is an <article>; the listing wrapper is a <section> inside <main>.

Documentation

Doc page as <article>, sidebar ToC as <aside>.

Forums

Thread as <article>, each reply as nested <article>.

SaaS dashboards

<main> holds the primary view; sidebar nav as <aside>; user settings panel as <aside> when sliding out.

Reader apps

Safari Reader, Firefox Reader, Pocket — all rely on <article> + <main> for content extraction.

SPAs

Keep <main> mounted; swap content inside it. SR users keep their place across route changes.
