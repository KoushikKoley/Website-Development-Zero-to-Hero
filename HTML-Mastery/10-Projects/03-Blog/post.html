The Long Road Between Idea and Shipped

Most software never ships. Of the software that ships, most is unrecognizable from the original idea. This essay is about the messy middle — the gap where most projects quietly die.

MK By Maya Kowalski · March 12, 2025 · 14 min read

A sketch from a project notebook. The line never goes where you expect.

The idea was simple. Three engineers, one designer, six weeks, a small internal tool that would save the support team forty hours a month. We wrote the spec in an afternoon. The team was excited. The work, we agreed, was the easy part.

It shipped fifteen months later. Two of the original engineers had left. The designer had moved to a different product. The tool did roughly half of what the spec described, and did a great many things the spec never mentioned. It saved the support team closer to twelve hours a month. Everyone agreed it was a success.

The Middle Is the Project

Conferences are full of beginnings and ends. There are talks about the initial insight — the moment the team saw the problem clearly. There are talks about the launch — the dashboard, the press cycle, the lessons distilled into three clean bullet points. There are very few talks about the eleven months in between, because the eleven months in between are mostly a series of awkward conversations and bad decisions that nobody wants to relive on stage.

But the middle is where the software actually gets made. The beginning is a story you tell afterwards. The end is a press release. The middle is a project.

The map is not the territory. The spec is not the software. The kickoff is not the work. The middle, where everything is uncertain and everyone is tired, is where the thing that ships gets built.

Three Things That Kill the Middle

After watching this project (and a dozen others) go sideways, I've come to believe the middle dies for three reasons. None of them are technical.

1. The team stops agreeing on what they're building

This one is invisible because it happens slowly. In the kickoff meeting everyone nodded at the same slides. By month four, each person is carrying a slightly different version of the project in their head — and none of those versions is the spec, because nobody has read the spec since week two.

The fix is mundane: write down what you're building, share it, and rewrite it together every two weeks. The artifact doesn't matter (a one-page brief is fine). The shared rewriting is what does the work.

2. The work gets quieter than the meetings

By month three, a project that started as a small team building a small thing has accumulated a weekly sync, a bi-weekly stakeholder review, a monthly all-hands update, and an asynchronous standup. The calendar fills up. The commits slow down. People start describing their work instead of doing it.

// A project health check I now run monthly:
const signalRatio = commitsThisWeek / meetingsThisWeek;
if (signalRatio < 1.5) {
  cancelSomething();  // anything
}

The arithmetic is harsh. A team of five in twelve hours of meetings a week is sixty person-hours of meeting. That's one and a half full-time engineers, gone. The fix isn't to abolish meetings. It's to notice when the ratio crosses the line, and cancel something — anything — to put it back.

3. Nobody is allowed to be tired

This is the one I see least discussed. The middle of a project is exhausting. The initial excitement is gone, the end is not yet in sight, and the work has gotten harder than anyone promised. Most teams have no language for this. They push through. They burn out. They ship a worse version of the thing and call it done.

The teams that ship the good version, in my experience, are the ones that name the tiredness out loud. "We are in the boring part. We are tired. The work is still good." Saying it doesn't fix it, but it stops the slow poisoning that comes from pretending everything is fine.

What to Do Instead

I don't have a framework. I have three habits, learned the hard way:

  • Write the brief again, every two weeks, with the team. Not because the spec changed — because the people did. They need to re-agree.
  • Measure meetings against commits. When the ratio crosses one meeting per commit, something is wrong and you should fix it before the next standup.
  • Name the tiredness. Say it out loud at the weekly sync. The team will exhale. The work will go better.

None of these will save your project. But they will make the middle less lonely. And the middle, I have come to believe, is where software either becomes real or quietly doesn't.

Coda

The internal tool I started this essay with saved the support team twelve hours a month, not forty. It is still running. The support team loves it. The engineer who finally shipped it was not on the original team. He read the brief, asked three questions, and built the half that mattered.

That, I think, is the real lesson. The road between idea and shipped is long, and most of the people who walk it aren't the people who started the journey. Ship something anyway. The next team will thank you for the parts you got right.
