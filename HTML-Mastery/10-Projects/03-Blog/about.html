About the publication

We write for the engineer in the middle of the project — too far in to quit, too tired to read another 2,000-word listicle.

Slow publishing, plain language, no tracking pixels. One essay every two weeks. Read on a Sunday, kept on a desk.

What we're trying to do

There is a strange gap in software writing. The introductory material is excellent — friendly, encouraging, free. The advanced material is excellent too — dense, technical, written by specialists. What's missing is the middle: the long, unglamorous years between "I learned the basics" and "I gave the keynote."

The Margin Notes exists to fill that gap. We publish essays about the parts of software engineering that don't get conference talks: the months of refinement, the meetings that should have been emails, the third rewrite that finally felt right, the morning you realized the spec was wrong all along and had to tell the team.

We believe these stories are useful. We believe they are best told slowly, in plain English, without selling anything. We believe the reader is intelligent, busy, and tired, and that the writer's job is to respect all three.

We publish under a Creative Commons license. We do not run ads. We do not track you. The weekly dispatch is plain text, sent on Sundays, and you can unsubscribe by replying with the word "stop."

Editorial guidelines

If you're thinking of pitching us, this is what we look for — and what we quietly reject.

Specific over general

"We rewrote the build system three times" beats "Thoughts on build systems." We want the story with names, dates, and the moment someone wanted to quit.

Plain language

If a smart friend who isn't an engineer can't follow your essay, we'll ask you to rewrite. If they can, but you used a buzzword to do it, we'll ask you to cut the buzzword.

No listicles

"Ten things every senior engineer should know" is a Twitter thread, not an essay. We publish arguments, observations, and stories. Sometimes those have numbers in them; the numbers are never the point.

Honest about failure

We don't publish victory laps. If the project failed, tell us why. If it succeeded, tell us what was harder than expected. We are uninterested in essays where everything went according to plan.

No tool worship

We don't run vendor posts, sponsored comparisons, or thinly disguised product launches. Tools come and go. The interesting writing is about the trade-offs underneath them.

Original thinking

If your draft restates something we've already published, we'll send it back. If it restates something we read on three other blogs last month, we'll send it back faster. Disagree with us. We like that.

How to pitch us

We commission roughly one essay a month from outside contributors. The process is slow because we'd rather publish one good piece than four forgettable ones.

  1. Send a one-paragraph pitch

    What's the argument, what's the story, and what will the reader walk away knowing they didn't know before? No attachments, no CVs, no portfolio links. Just the paragraph.

    Pitch window: always open · Response time: 2–3 weeks

  2. We commission, with a brief

    If we say yes, we send you a short brief — length, deadline, and a paragraph on what we think the essay is. You can push back on any of it. The brief is a starting point, not a contract.

    Standard length: 1,800–3,500 words · Fee: paid

  3. First draft

    Send us a complete first draft, not an outline. We can't tell if a piece works until we read it end to end. We will read it within a week and send comments.

    Turnaround: 7 days from receipt

  4. Two rounds of edits

    We do a structural edit first (does the argument work, is anything missing, what should go) and a line edit second (clarity, sentences, rhythm). You always have the final say on tone and substance.

    Two rounds, rarely three

  5. Publication

    We publish roughly three weeks after the final draft is approved. We send you the link the night before, and you keep the copyright. We publish under CC BY 4.0 — you can republish anywhere, with attribution.

    Payment: net 30 from publication
